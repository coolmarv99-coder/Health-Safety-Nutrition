Opening vignette

“The Room Has Risks Too”

Maya enters the classroom before children arrive. At first, she sees colorful shelves, puzzles, blocks, books, dramatic-play materials, art supplies, and a cozy corner.

After reading this week’s chapter, however, she begins looking differently. Are the scissors appropriate for the children’s age? Could small toy pieces become choking hazards? Is that cord near the listening center a tripping risk?

She also notices what has been done well: cleaning supplies are locked away, the water table is emptied and covered, and furniture is arranged so teachers can see across the room.

Young children climb, reach, taste, pull, run, dump, hide, and test what things can do. Their curiosity is essential to learning—but it means educators have to anticipate risks before children discover them the hard way.

When a teacher moves one shelf slightly to create a clearer traffic path, Maya writes: Safety is built into the environment before children ever walk through the door.

Safety Scan / Indoor Case File

Scan Maya’s classroom

Classify each observation. Unsafe means action is warranted now; acceptable means the condition supports safety; investigate means the facts are insufficient for a responsible judgment.

1. A clear spray bottle with no label is on the counter above the art sink.

2. Low open shelving separates centers while preserving sight lines across the room.

3. A basket contains smooth wooden pieces that some children use as pretend food.

You need to know their size and condition—and the ages, abilities, and mouthing behavior of children who can access them.

4. A tall movable shelf is not anchored, and children sometimes pull themselves up using its edge.

5. Child-safe scissors are available at the supervised art table.

Consider which children are present, whether the scissors truly fit their developmental level, and how access is supervised.

6. Cleaning products remain in original labeled containers inside a locked cabinet inaccessible to children.
