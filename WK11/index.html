Maya’s inclusion insight

Good planning helps children be children first.

Maya reads a sample Individualized Health Plan for a child with severe allergies. It lists symptoms, emergency steps, medication instructions, family contacts, health-provider information, and classroom precautions.

At first, the plan feels formal. Then she imagines the child at snack with friends—laughing, reaching for food, and trusting the adults. The document becomes personal: adults have thought ahead, staff do not have to guess, and the child belongs.

Children with asthma, diabetes, seizures, mobility or feeding needs, allergies, and other chronic conditions may need individualized planning. Inclusion requires communication, training, documentation, collaboration, and clear responsibility—not treating a child as fragile.

OBSERVEKnow the child’s functioning, strengths, and signs

Week 11 principle: Individualization is not a favor or a lowered expectation. It is purposeful planning that gives a child safe, meaningful access to the program.

For each situation, select the information staff should consult or use first. A real plan may connect several parts; choose the best starting point.

1. During outdoor play, Ari pauses twice but says he feels fine. Staff need to determine whether this differs from his usual activity pattern.

2. Samira is joining snack. Her plan specifies a safe food substitution and the supervision that supports independent eating.

3. After an accidental exposure, Leo develops the specific breathing and skin signs listed as an emergency in his allergy plan.

4. Maya notices that a child’s rescue medication will expire next week and the plan must be updated before a field trip.

5. A teacher writes, “Nia was lazy today.” The team needs a better record before deciding whether the plan needs review.

A useful replacement would describe what Nia did, how long it lasted, and how this compared with her usual functioning.

6. A child can carry her own inhaler pouch during a classroom transition, as allowed by the plan, with an educator checking that it remains accessible.

A care plan promotes belonging when…

Prepared

Specific routine and emergency steps, trained people, accessible supplies, and current contacts replace guesswork with readiness.

Collaborative

Families, educators, and health or service providers contribute different expertise and revise the plan as the child’s needs change.

Inclusive

Supports protect health while preserving dignity, confidentiality, relationships, meaningful participation, and growing independence.
